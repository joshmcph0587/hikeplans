Before you buy anything

Six things worth knowing first

Most first-kit money gets wasted on the wrong things. This is the short version of what actually matters.

Base weight is the number people mean

Base weight is everything in your pack minus food, water, and fuel — the stuff that doesn't shrink as you hike. It's the only figure that lets two kits be compared honestly.

Under 20 lb is comfortable. Under 15 is light. Under 10 takes real money or real discipline.

A "20-degree bag" is not comfortable at 20°

Most bags are labeled with their lower limit — the point a warm sleeper survives, not sleeps. The comfort rating is usually 8–10° warmer.

If you want to actually sleep well at 30°F, shop 20°F-rated. Read the ISO comfort number, not the name on the stuff sack.

Your pad matters as much as your bag

Cold comes up from the ground, and down crushes flat underneath you where it can't insulate. A warm bag on a summer pad still sleeps cold.

For alpine nights near freezing you want R-value 4.5 or better. Below about R 2.5 is a summer-only pad, whatever the bag says.

The Big Three own the budget

Shelter, pack, and sleep system are roughly 60% of your weight and 70% of your cost. Everything else is rounding.

Spend here, in this order: sleep system first (it's the safety item and fit matters most), then shelter, then pack — buy the pack last, once you know what volume you actually need.

Don't buy it all at once

Rent the tent. Borrow the pack. Buy the sleep system. Go out for one night close to home and find out what you actually hate before you commit.

Wait for the sales — 20% off one full-price item runs a few times a year and it's worth timing your most expensive purchase around it.

Food: about 1.5 to 2 lb per day

Aim for 120+ calories per ounce so you're carrying energy, not packaging. Roughly 2,500–3,500 kcal a day on big-elevation days.

Weigh and bag every day separately at home. It's the only way to know what you're carrying, and it stops you from hauling four days of food on a three-day trip.
